DAY 15 / MOVEMENT 02

Reasoning with evidence and alternatives

Make a manageable decision inspectable through evidence, alternatives, assumptions, a small practical test and revision.

In this guide

PurposeWorked exampleStudio stepsTeaching and evidenceReview and accessOriginal readingNext stepPractical resources ↗

Expanded studio guide · 28 October 2026

What you will practise

Make a manageable decision inspectable through evidence, alternatives, assumptions, a small practical test and revision.

A reasoning map makes the relationship between observations and a choice available for review. It should contain plausible alternatives and the conditions each needs. A model's explanation is another proposal to inspect against sources. The learner's attraction to an option can remain part of the record alongside resources and consequences, without becoming evidence that the option will work.

A worked example

Illustrative example: a group is choosing between an outdoor music rehearsal and a smaller indoor session. The fictional brief includes limited equipment, uncertain weather and one participant's need for a quieter setting. Rather than declaring the outdoor option best because it sounds inviting, the learner could identify missing information, rehearse the setup and compare the available alternatives.

Make, test and revise

  1. Write the desired result, affected people, available records, resources and constraints. Separate observations, interpretations and preferences. Choose a decision with manageable consequences so a small test can inform it without creating an unnecessary commitment.
  2. Prepare at least three plausible approaches and state the assumptions behind each. Use a table, spoken comparison or visual map. Ask whose perspective is missing and what source or conversation would help include it.
  3. Review a peer or model-generated recommendation against the actual brief. Identify an unsupported detail or omitted condition and request a revision. Record your provisional choice, confidence in it and the reasons for that confidence.
  4. Test one important assumption through a rehearsal or small encounter. Compare expectation and observation, revise the decision or next question, and retain the earlier reasoning so the change is understandable to another person.

Teach it to another adult

Present the map to an adult peer and invite an alternative explanation. Summarise their view faithfully before choosing an observation that could help distinguish it from your current interpretation.

Keep evidence someone can inspect

  • A decision brief separating observations, interpretations and preferences.
  • Alternative approaches with assumptions, sources and a provisional judgement.
  • A practical test, revised confidence and a reasoned next step.

Try a changed condition

Change a resource or include a newly affected person. Ask which assumption and part of the decision must be revisited. A strong answer can explain why an earlier choice was reasonable under its earlier conditions.

Reflect on the result

  • Which observation most affected the choice?
  • What would change your current judgement?
  • Whose interests need another account?

Adapt the encounter

Offer a short spoken comparison or three option cards instead of a dense table. Keep the first decision ordinary. A learner may use a fictional civic scenario rather than an unresolved personal disagreement.

Take it further

A builder can structure the map as linked claims and tests. A creative learner can narrate each option from an affected person's viewpoint, then return the newly noticed condition to the shared decision record.

The original daily reading

Preserved reading from the 14 October 2026 course, PDF page 15. The studio guide above adds practical detail; it does not replace this source.

Reasoning connects information with an inference or decision. Today's objective is to make that connection inspectable, compare alternative explanations and learn from what happens next. The learner uses a real question with manageable consequences, such as choosing a learning project, arranging a creative event or improving a familiar routine.

The starting brief records the result sought, people affected, available knowledge, resources and conditions. The learner separates observations from interpretations through labelled records. They ask their Aura for several plausible approaches, then examine the assumptions each approach requires. A plan receives more attention when its conditions fit the actual situation.

The studio task builds a reasoning map. It contains the question, relevant records, candidate explanations, consequences, unresolved questions and a chosen next test. Technical learners create a structured form; creative learners draw or narrate the relationships; civic learners examine whose perspective has been included. The different representations return to the same decision.

A model-generated explanation is reviewed against the source material. The learner identifies an unsupported detail, an omitted condition or a useful connection, then requests a revision. The accepted result includes the source links and the reason for the chosen approach. This becomes an example of correction for the personal model and a teaching artefact for later use.

The outside exercise tests a small part of the decision. Someone choosing a public workshop may try the explanation with one newcomer. Someone selecting a new tool may rehearse its main function. The learner compares the observed result with the expectation and records what changed. An informative failure supplies a revised question and a more useful next step.

The reflection asks where attraction, repulsion and identity affected the decision. A learner may favour an option because it fits an admired best self, or avoid another because it recalls an unpleasant experience. Those influences belong in the reasoning record, alongside practical constraints. The aim is a decision the person understands well enough to revise.

Teaching practice presents the reasoning map to a peer and invites a different interpretation. The trainee summarises the alternative faithfully, then identifies which observation would help distinguish the possibilities. Evidence includes the brief, alternatives, source checks, practical test and revised decision. This develops the feedback loop described in the mastermind and alignment sources: choose a useful result, compare what happened with what was intended and improve the next attempt. [S06, pp. 2-4; S12, pp. 23-24]

The learner records a confidence estimate and the reasons behind it, then revisits the estimate after the practical test. The comparison shows which observation changed their judgement and supplies another example of evidence-informed revision.

Source: S06 Mastermind Swarmwise; S12 Joyful Responsible Abundance Human and SI Alignment Revised 11 October 2026.
