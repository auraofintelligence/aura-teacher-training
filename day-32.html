DAY 32 / MOVEMENT 04

Plural values and shared decisions

Represent plural values faithfully and design a shared decision process that identifies affected people and actual decision rights.

In this guide

PurposeWorked exampleStudio stepsTeaching and evidenceReview and accessOriginal readingNext stepPractical resources ↗

Expanded studio guide · 28 October 2026

What you will practise

Represent plural values faithfully and design a shared decision process that identifies affected people and actual decision rights.

A shared map should preserve different worthwhile lives rather than compressing them into a single score. The source’s Joyful Responsible Abundance questions offer a lens for examining enjoyment, consequences and widely available capability, while people retain distinct answers. Separate a value, a factual constraint, a predicted consequence and a decision authority. A model can organise these relationships without receiving the right to decide for everyone. Use a fictional civic situation so learners can practise disagreement without presenting a class vote as an actual community mandate.

A worked example

Illustrative example: a shared learning room might host evening music, quiet study or a mixed timetable. One role values creative contact, another a dependable quiet space and another accessible transport home. A fictional room steward has the decision responsibility. The exercise compares options and missing evidence; it does not claim that averaging the participants’ preferences produces a fair or authorised result.

Make, test and revise

  1. Give each participant a value statement and a chance to rewrite it. Record its author, meaning and circumstances, then ask what would misrepresent that concern.
  2. Create three feasible options using the same fictional resource limits. Map potential benefits, burdens and uncertainties against every attributed account, without silently inventing missing facts.
  3. Add a perspective overlooked initially. Revise one option and ask that contributor whether the revision addresses the actual concern or only changes its wording.
  4. Name the decision holder, participation offered, reasons to record and review date. Preserve dissent and a question requiring further evidence instead of forcing apparent consensus.

Teach it to another adult

Invite a newcomer to state one concern and follow it through an option. Ask where it enters the record and who can decide what happens next.

Keep evidence someone can inspect

  • An attributed value map separating values from facts and authority.
  • Three options with consequences, uncertainty and a revised overlooked perspective.
  • A decision-process record retaining reasons, dissent and review responsibility.

Try a changed condition

Move the method to a personal choice between a demanding project and time for relationships. Check whether the learner can represent their own competing values without declaring one universally superior.

Reflect on the result

  • Whose concern was absent from the first account?
  • Which consequence needs evidence before it can guide the choice?
  • Does the participation method match the decision rights actually available?

Adapt the encounter

Use voice, pictures, written statements or a facilitator’s returned summary. Allow private preparation and a supporter chosen by the adult, preserving their own wording.

Take it further

Present the same options through a short dramatic scene. Compare what the scene reveals with the written map and check that added emotion has not invented someone’s position.

The original daily reading

Preserved reading from the 14 October 2026 course, PDF page 32. The studio guide above adds practical detail; it does not replace this source.

People bring different worthwhile lives into a shared setting. Today's objective is to represent those differences faithfully and find a decision process that respects the people affected. The learner works with a civic scenario where several values deserve attention, such as the use of a shared room, an environmental intervention or a community event.

Each participant describes what matters to them and why. One may value quiet access, another creative activity, another care, another ecological protection and another a dependable service. Their Auras help organise the accounts, relevant sources and practical conditions. The shared map retains the author and context of each value statement.

The decision studio develops several options and examines their consequences across the different accounts. A specialist team retrieves sources, models resource demands, proposes designs and challenges assumptions. The humans review whether each option represents their concerns. An unresolved difference receives a named question and a route for further conversation or evidence.

The October alignment source connects human self-alignment with the values that guide growing artificial capability. Its three recurring questions ask whether life became more worth living, whether consequences were treated responsibly and whether useful capability became more abundant and widely shared. The learner uses those questions as a shared lens while retaining the diversity of individual answers. [S12, p. 4]

Technical builders inspect how the value map enters the models and how a decision returns to the affected people. Philosophical learners examine assumptions about fairness, freedom, responsibility and belonging. Creative learners turn the competing possibilities into a short scene so the group experiences their human consequences. Civic teachers identify the actual decision holder and the form of participation offered.

The reflection examines a personal conflict between values. A learner may want adventure and stability, solitude and intimacy, speed and care. They describe the conditions under which each matters and use the same method to make a small personal decision. This keeps civic reasoning connected to lived complexity.

Teaching practice invites a newcomer to state a value in their own words, then checks whether the shared map preserves it. The trainee asks them to review one option and identify a consequence they want considered. Evidence includes the attributed value map, options, source pack, modelled consequences, unresolved questions and decision process. The capability built today is plural alignment in practice: different meanings remain legible while people and Auras seek useful ways to act together.

A participant proposes a value the first map overlooked. The learner revises one option to include that perspective and checks the consequence with the contributor. The exercise shows how a shared decision becomes richer through further participation.
