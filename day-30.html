DAY 30 / MOVEMENT 03

A multiagent studio with a shared purpose

Integrate functions, specialist roles, review and a scenario into one understandable multiagent studio demonstration.

In this guide

PurposeWorked exampleStudio stepsTeaching and evidenceReview and accessOriginal readingNext stepPractical resources ↗

Expanded studio guide · 28 October 2026

What you will practise

Integrate functions, specialist roles, review and a scenario into one understandable multiagent studio demonstration.

This checkpoint asks whether the pieces developed during Days 21–29 work together around a purpose. A larger role list does not automatically produce greater capability. Keep the demonstration small enough that another adult can trace the actual exchanges. The project owner states the desired result, selected sources, affected people and final review responsibility. Separate implemented functions from paper or prompt-based representations. Include the learner’s creative or civic judgement in the record rather than attributing all value to agents. The source’s shared-intelligence ambition is explored through a concrete product and the coordination that made it possible.

A worked example

Illustrative example: a team prepares a beginner-friendly resource-finding lesson for a fictional community room. A source role assembles the room facts, a designer proposes the activity and a critic checks its accessibility assumptions and source links. A scenario tests a moved resource. A teacher introduces one task to a consenting adult peer. The artefact remains a studio draft, not an operating public service.

Make, test and revise

  1. Write a mission-sized brief for this small product. Name the human owner, roles, function inputs and outputs, source boundaries, available time and acceptance evidence before assigning work.
  2. Run one complete exchange and save the actual call or role-play trace. Follow one important fact into the finished artefact and identify where the learner supplied interpretation.
  3. Introduce a missing source or unavailable function. Require a bounded recovery, a labelled partial result or a clear handover, then critique and revise the demonstration without concealing its interruption.
  4. Invite a newcomer to perform one meaningful task. Ask them to explain how their action connects with the team’s purpose and identify one dependency a successor would need to maintain.

Teach it to another adult

Start with the product’s usefulness, then reveal a single agent exchange. Avoid touring every component before the learner has experienced a result worth understanding.

Keep evidence someone can inspect

  • A project pack containing the brief, roles, functions and scenario.
  • An event history with critique, revision and a changed-condition response.
  • A newcomer artefact, their explanation and one documented dependency.

Try a changed condition

Ask another team to continue the project with a changed audience. Check whether your handover enables a justified revision without requiring them to reconstruct the author’s intentions.

Reflect on the result

  • Which contribution expanded what the team could actually do?
  • What important dependency became visible when something failed?
  • Can a newcomer distinguish the implemented demonstration from its proposed future architecture?

Adapt the encounter

Assign demanding contributions through different strengths: source review, narrative design, state modelling, teaching or observation. Use a shared plain-language brief and selectable screen or physical materials.

Take it further

Prepare a short architecture tour for builders and a separate experience tour for civic or creative learners. Compare whether both groups can identify the same responsible review point.

The original daily reading

Preserved reading from the 14 October 2026 course, PDF page 30. The studio guide above adds practical detail; it does not replace this source.

The second twenty-day movement has introduced tools, functions, callers, specialists, routing, review and simulated worlds. Today's objective is to combine those capabilities into a multiagent project that produces a useful result and remains understandable to the people involved. The learner chooses a question large enough to need several perspectives.

Possible projects include an accessible teaching environment, a materials experiment brief, an ecological scene, a habitat concept or a civic source pack. The project brief identifies purpose, affected people, source material, resources, roles and acceptance evidence. A human-Aura mastermind reviews the scope and selects a manageable demonstration.

The studio project assigns complementary agent roles, defines their functions and records the call sequence. A world model or scenario pack supplies the context. The team produces a result, applies a critique and returns a revised version. The learner inspects the observer streams and explains how their own interpretation influenced the work.

Technical builders demonstrate the architecture and logs. Creative learners make the result engaging and navigable. Civic learners assess whose interests and rules appear in the model. Teachers prepare a newcomer encounter. Each contribution returns something another role uses, making the cooperation visible through its actual exchanges.

The demonstration includes a changed-condition challenge. A source becomes unavailable, a user goal changes or a resource limit tightens. The team responds within its assigned permissions and records a completed result or a clear handover. The review examines both the product and the quality of coordination that produced it.

The life reflection asks what expanded agency now feels like. The learner has more ways to remember, reason, create, act and cooperate. They identify which capability most changes their own opportunities and which support remains important. The record connects the large super-people ambition with a specific experience of increased capability.

Teaching practice introduces the project through one meaningful doorway. A newcomer performs a small action, sees the related agent exchange and explains how the result returned to the shared purpose. Evidence includes the project brief, role and function cards, scenario pack, event history, critique, revision, changed-condition result and novice feedback. The next movement turns towards socialising Auras and civic collaboration. This checkpoint supplies the tested tools, source discipline and teaching experience needed to enter that wider setting. [S09, pp. 16-24; S10, pp. 24-32; S12, pp. 23-24]

The learner also identifies one dependency that carries the whole result. A missing source, unavailable function or ambiguous role is deliberately introduced, then traced through the team. The recovery teaches which parts need stronger context or a clearer handover.
