DAY 05 / MOVEMENT 01

Embodiment avatar and body real estate

Choose an editable avatar and interaction arrangement that feels recognisable and supports the learner's intended activities.

In this guide

PurposeWorked exampleStudio stepsTeaching and evidenceReview and accessOriginal readingNext stepPractical resources ↗

Expanded studio guide · 28 October 2026

What you will practise

Choose an editable avatar and interaction arrangement that feels recognisable and supports the learner's intended activities.

The avatar is a selected representation with a purpose, not a complete measurement of the person. The source offers simple two-dimensional, stylised and more detailed routes. Body real estate extends this into interaction design: consider which controls are comfortable, which areas should remain free and which tasks belong on a room display or in voice interaction.

A worked example

Illustrative example: Priya wants an avatar for rehearsing a community presentation. A simple character with chosen clothing and a seated option could be sufficient. Priya places slide selection on a handheld control rather than a wrist device, because both hands need to remain available for demonstrating an object. This is a proposed design preference, not a tested accessibility result.

Make, test and revise

  1. Write the avatar's immediate purpose and choose a level of detail. Start with a sketch or an available editor. Use sample dimensions or a generic figure where personal images and measurements are unnecessary.
  2. Demonstrate looking towards an item, moving or changing viewpoint, and selecting a record. Record what is actually possible in the chosen medium. A paper storyboard can show the intended sequence without claiming implemented interaction.
  3. Make a body-and-environment control map. Compare handheld, voice, wrist and room-display options for one task. Note comfort, visibility, attention and areas the person prefers to leave free, then choose a provisional arrangement.
  4. Observe one ordinary device interaction and revise the proposal around it. Label measured details, manual edits and aspirational features separately. Give the revised avatar and control map a version so a collaborator can follow the change.

Teach it to another adult

Ask an adult newcomer what they want their representation to support. Help them change one feature and explain its purpose. Check whether they can describe the three actions without needing your preferred appearance or control.

Keep evidence someone can inspect

  • An avatar version with a stated purpose and provenance for its details.
  • A three-action demonstration or clearly labelled storyboard.
  • A comparison of control locations with the learner's reasons and revision.

Try a changed condition

Move the chosen activity from a desk to a shared room. Ask which controls, visibility and free-space requirements change. Revise the design before assuming that a comfortable desk arrangement transfers.

Reflect on the result

  • Which detail makes the representation useful to its person?
  • What source supports each physical detail?
  • Which interaction interrupts the activity it should support?

Adapt the encounter

A seated sketch, narrated storyboard or keyboard route is sufficient. No scan, bodily contact, headset or personal photograph is required. Let the learner identify comfort conditions rather than infer them from the avatar.

Take it further

Compare the same action on the body, in the room and through voice. A builder can document input events; a creative participant can rehearse the encounter and identify where attention shifts.

The original daily reading

Preserved reading from the 14 October 2026 course, PDF page 5. The studio guide above adds practical detail; it does not replace this source.

An avatar gives the learner a point of view and a way to inhabit a representation. Today's objective is a form that feels recognisable, supports the learner's chosen activities and remains editable. A simple two-dimensional avatar, a stylised three-dimensional character and a detailed body representation provide selectable routes. The learner chooses what level of detail matters for their current purpose.

The AuraOZ progression begins with body measurements and an animation rig, then adds appearance, facial features, clothing and renders. It later introduces a body real-estate overlay for accessories, touch points and fast access to information. This sequence supplies today's practical design: representation first, meaningful interaction second. [S04, Days 2 and 8]

The studio task builds or customises a basic avatar and demonstrates three ordinary actions: looking towards something, moving through a space and selecting an Aura record. An external scan or movement recording uses a stated purpose and the learner's permissions. Measurements, images and manual edits retain their provenance. The learner checks appearance, comfort, cultural expression and the experience of seeing themselves through the chosen form.

Body real estate is explored as a design space. The learner maps where devices or controls would be comfortable and which areas they prefer to leave free. An eyewear interface, voice command, handheld control, wrist device and room display offer different interaction patterns. Accessibility preferences appear on the same map. Technical builders inspect input events; other learners focus on effort and recognisability.

The avatar also supports the past, present and best-self lenses. A person may represent current movement, a remembered performance and a future practice through different animations or environments. The map labels what each representation expresses. It carries the learner's intention alongside the source measurements, so another collaborator understands the design choice.

The outside-life exercise is to observe one everyday interaction with a device. The learner notices posture, attention, interruptions and the way the interface enters the activity. They then redesign that encounter using their avatar and chosen control. A lighting routine, reminder, musical cue or navigation prompt gives the experiment a clear purpose.

Teaching practice is an avatar introduction with a newcomer. The trainee invites a representation preference, explains the available tools and helps the person change one feature. Evidence includes the avatar version, the three-action demonstration, the body-real-estate map and the person's feedback. The exercise makes personal computing embodied and teaches the importance of fitting tools to the actual human encounter. It also prepares the nested Aura geometry introduced tomorrow.

A final design comparison places the same control on the body, in the room and in a voice interaction. The learner identifies which arrangement suits a chosen activity and records the reason, giving the next builder a concrete comfort requirement.

Source: S04 AuraOZ Learning Progression and Thought Experiment 2017.
