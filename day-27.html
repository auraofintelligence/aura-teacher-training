DAY 27 / MOVEMENT 03

Building a world model

Build a small world model whose states, permitted actions and uncertainties support one inspectable event sequence.

In this guide

PurposeWorked exampleStudio stepsTeaching and evidenceReview and accessOriginal readingNext stepPractical resources ↗

Expanded studio guide · 28 October 2026

What you will practise

Build a small world model whose states, permitted actions and uncertainties support one inspectable event sequence.

A useful world model is selective. It represents enough of a setting to answer the chosen question and marks what remains unknown. The source separates cognitive, social and physical aspects, but the learner need not build an entire simulated society. Begin with one room, garden or studio and a tractable visitor task. Distinguish an observed feature, a sourced rule, an inference and an invented scene detail. A state change should be inspectable: what was true before, what action occurred and what is recorded afterwards. A model maintainer owns corrections; a scene alone confers no authority to change the represented place.

A worked example

Illustrative example: a fictional learning room contains a welcome desk, two shelves, a clear path and a shared tablet. The task is to find an approved beginner guide. A paper model records which shelf holds it and whether the tablet is available. A visitor borrowing the guide changes its availability. The scenario does not claim actual room measurements or access certification.

Make, test and revise

  1. Choose one visitor question and draw only the features needed to answer it. Create records for layout, resources, actions, people, rules and unresolved details, each with a provenance label.
  2. Describe a starting state and three events: arrive, request a resource and record the result. Use cards or a small local data demonstration to show each state before and after.
  3. Make the usual doorway unavailable or move the guide. Test whether the proposed route changes and whether the model asks about an alternative it cannot establish.
  4. Identify one uncertain detail that matters to the result. Draft a permission-aware observation plan naming who could check it, what would be measured and where a correction would be recorded.

Teach it to another adult

Let another adult choose an action in the scene. Ask them which cues supported the choice and which knowledge you assumed they already had.

Keep evidence someone can inspect

  • A purpose-led model with provenance on facts, assumptions and invented details.
  • A three-event record showing state changes and responsible actions.
  • A changed-condition result plus one specific observation question.

Try a changed condition

Rebuild the same resource-finding task in a fictional outdoor learning area. Ask a peer to identify new ecological or temporal conditions that the indoor model did not represent.

Reflect on the result

  • What information is necessary for this question and what is unnecessary detail?
  • Which state changed because of the action?
  • What real observation would distinguish two plausible model assumptions?

Adapt the encounter

Provide a tabletop layout, text list or narrated scene alongside any spatial model. XR remains optional; preserve the same states and questions across formats.

Take it further

Introduce opening times or a seasonal condition. Test how the model represents time without treating a past observation as permanently true.

The original daily reading

Preserved reading from the 14 October 2026 course, PDF page 27. The studio guide above adds practical detail; it does not replace this source.

A world model represents a setting, its objects, people, rules, resources and changing conditions. Today's objective is to build a small environment that gives an Aura a meaningful place to reason and act. The learner chooses a familiar scene before adding more difficult relationships.

The scene may be a room, garden, studio, community hub or remembered meeting place. The learner describes its layout, objects, possible actions, occupants and relevant norms. The model records which details are observed, supplied by a source, inferred or imagined. A practical question gives the environment its purpose, such as teaching a new visitor how to find a resource.

The studio task creates a simple scene and runs one event sequence. The system retrieves the current state, processes an interaction, updates the relevant states, applies changing conditions, saves the outcome and reviews whether the intended result occurred. Technical builders implement the state changes. Creative learners shape the experience. Civic learners identify rules and people affected by an action.

The source life-simulation paper separates cognitive, social and physical submodels, then combines them in a simulation step. It also introduces daily routines, cultural influences, cycles and seasons. The new lesson starts with a tractable scene so those ideas become visible through an actual event rather than remaining a large abstract list. [S10, pp. 13-24]

The class changes one condition. A doorway becomes unavailable, a resource moves or a visitor has a different access need. The Aura revises its proposed action and explains the changed route. The learner checks whether the scene contains enough information to support the decision and adds missing context where appropriate.

The reflection asks how the learner's own environment shapes capability. A task that feels simple in a familiar room may become difficult in another setting. They identify the cues, relationships and supports that make their surroundings intelligible. A best-self scenario then describes the conditions under which they would like to act in an unfamiliar place.

Teaching practice introduces the scene to a newcomer, invites them to choose an action and asks what they noticed. The trainee observes which cues helped and which assumptions were invisible. Evidence includes the world description, state sequence, changed-condition result, source labels and learner feedback. Tomorrow's three-observer model will deepen this work by separating the whole environment, the part a character notices and the interpretation of another observer.

The learner labels a scene detail that remains uncertain and designs a small observation to resolve it. This turns a world-model gap into a fieldwork question and links the simulated environment with its living source.

Source: S10 MoE Extended.
