DAY 51 / MOVEMENT 06

A solar system mission and its civic purpose

Connect a solar-system instrument mission with a scientific question, an explicit resource model and a continuing civic purpose.

In this guide

PurposeWorked exampleStudio stepsTeaching and evidenceReview and accessOriginal readingNext stepPractical resources ↗

Expanded studio guide · 28 October 2026

What you will practise

Connect a solar-system instrument mission with a scientific question, an explicit resource model and a continuing civic purpose.

Begin with what a mission would help someone understand or decide. Then ask which observation and instrument could inform that question. A simulator supplies a design environment, not proof that a mission is feasible or funded. Preserve epochs and units for numerical inputs. Consider who maintains the record, how results are shared and how access decisions affect people beyond the team designing the instrument.

A worked example

A fictional mission compares one specialised observation node with three simpler complementary nodes. The paper budget allocates twelve power tokens: the first architecture uses nine and the second uses three per node. Both fit the illustrative total, but the distributed option adds communication and coordination dependencies. Token arithmetic cannot establish actual engineering performance. An Earth-analogue data task can first test whether the complementary observations improve the intended decision.

Make, test and revise

  1. Choose a focal body or orbital setting from the source studio. Write the scientific question, civic purpose and observation needed before selecting instruments.
  2. Prepare two architecture cards with assumed mass, power, data, contact and maintenance demands. Label invented values and retain source epochs for factual inputs.
  3. Design a small Earth-analogue comparison using supplied observations or synthetic data. Identify the result that would favour one architecture under the stated assumptions.
  4. Add a fictional future entry-rule card with jurisdiction, disclosure choice, review and appeal. Examine its consequences without presenting the Aura Passport hypothesis as current travel authority.

Teach it to another adult

Invite an adult peer to choose an instrument and explain what its observation would resolve. Ask them to distinguish a mission purpose from an instrument's capabilities.

Keep evidence someone can inspect

  • A mission card linking question, observation, instrument rationale and affected decision-makers.
  • Two resource models with explicit assumptions, maintenance responsibilities and a discriminating analogue test.
  • A stewardship and fictional-rule review addressing access, disclosure, appeal and continuing public learning.

Try a changed condition

Remove one distributed node or a contact window. Explain how the remaining observations change the mission's useful result and next design decision.

Reflect on the result

  • What decision would the observation actually inform?
  • Which resource assumption most affects the architecture?
  • Who continues stewardship after the initial demonstration?

Adapt the encounter

Allow a narrated mission, tactile architecture cards or a plain table. Instrument choice can be argued through evidence without operating an immersive orbital display.

Take it further

Write a public-learning brief that makes the mission's uncertainty interesting and gives another investigator a concrete observation or comparison to contribute.

The original daily reading

Preserved reading from the 14 October 2026 course, PDF page 51. The studio guide above adds practical detail; it does not replace this source.

Solar-system stewardship begins with a purpose, a place and the people or other beings affected by the work. Today's objective is to design an instrument or habitat-support mission with a scientific question and a civic reason for pursuing it. The learner chooses a focal body or orbital setting in Virtual Solar Swarm and explains the connection to their wider mission.

The source project traces its origin to a June 2019 student sensor-package challenge in Kolhapur. Its current studio includes a body catalogue, orbit views, launch windows, instrument choices, design budgets and network behaviour. The learner uses that studio as a design environment and records the source and epoch of any numerical inputs. Project Atlas: Virtual Solar Swarm

The mission studio produces a mission card: focal body, question, observation, instrument rationale, operating setting, mass, power, data, communication and maintenance. The learner identifies the result an instrument would need to return and the way it informs a decision. A material question might connect with habitat manufacturing; an environmental question might connect with a long-term observation network.

Technical builders check units and resource dependencies. A specialist agent proposes instruments, a critic challenges the assumptions and a mentor identifies the next knowledge needed. Creative learners make the mission intelligible through a scene. Civic learners examine resource ownership, access to results, environmental consequences and the people responsible for decisions.

The group compares two architectures, such as a single specialised node and several complementary nodes. Each receives an explicit design budget and a maintenance scenario. The learner records which assumptions make an architecture attractive and which measurement or calculation would distinguish the options. An Earth-analogue experiment supplies a practical first step.

The civic reflection considers how movement, entry and belonging might work across future communities. The Aura Passport hypothesis invites jurisdictions to define a minimum public-profile completion level under Truth Engine conditions for entry or visa decisions, with participant-controlled disclosure. The learner designs a fictional future rule card with purpose, jurisdiction, conditions, review and appeal, then examines its consequences through plural values.

Teaching practice introduces the mission through its question, then lets a newcomer choose one instrument and explain the relationship to the result sought. Evidence includes the mission card, source epochs, two architectures, budget assumptions, maintenance scenario and Earth-analogue plan. The learner enters civic space stewardship through a demanding whole-system design: science, matter, infrastructure, governance, culture and care meet in one reviewed proposal.

A stewardship review asks who maintains the mission record across years and changing teams. The learner identifies a source archive, update responsibility and public-learning route, extending the design beyond launch into continuing civic usefulness.
