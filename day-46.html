DAY 46 / MOVEMENT 05

Ecology regeneration and living systems

Model one ecological relationship through sourced quantities, lived knowledge and a comparison of possible changes.

In this guide

PurposeWorked exampleStudio stepsTeaching and evidenceReview and accessOriginal readingNext stepPractical resources ↗

Expanded studio guide · 28 October 2026

What you will practise

Model one ecological relationship through sourced quantities, lived knowledge and a comparison of possible changes.

An ecological model helps a learner inspect relationships rather than merely decorate a place with data. Choose a boundary that fits the question and keep exchanges beyond that boundary visible. Distinguish stocks, flows and observations taken at a particular time. Local experience can reveal a missing season or dependency; its attribution and meaning should remain visible alongside the model's numbers and assumptions.

A worked example

A fictional shared garden holds forty units of stored water. A paper scenario adds ten units and removes twelve, leaving thirty-eight. That arithmetic describes the declared storage model, not plant health, soil moisture or a real watering recommendation. Another gardener explains that shade changes across the year. The group now has a better question: which observations would show whether the same practice has different effects under different conditions?

Make, test and revise

  1. Choose water, food, habitat or a waste stream and draw its boundary. Identify living participants, responsible people and flows crossing the boundary.
  2. Create a small source ledger for each quantity or relationship. Label synthetic values, dated observations, local accounts and unresolved gaps separately.
  3. Compare two paper interventions under identical stated assumptions. Trace resource demands and consequences, including effects that the simple model cannot represent.
  4. Invite a peer to represent a local contributor. Add a seasonal or cultural relationship, then revise the next observation rather than inventing certainty.

Teach it to another adult

Use an everyday meal to trace one resource through the model. Ask another adult to identify where the account stops and another relationship begins.

Keep evidence someone can inspect

  • A bounded ecological relationship with attributed living, material and human dependencies.
  • Two intervention scenarios preserving units, dates, synthetic assumptions and information gaps.
  • A revision showing how another person's knowledge changed the next measurement or question.

Try a changed condition

Apply the garden model to a habitat with limited resupply. Identify which flows become critical and which Earth-based assumptions no longer hold.

Reflect on the result

  • What important relationship lies outside the boundary?
  • Which observation could distinguish the intervention options?
  • Whose knowledge changed the model's next question?

Adapt the encounter

Offer movable flow cards, a spoken resource story or an accessible table. Field participation is optional and requires the host's actual permission.

Take it further

Propose a separately scoped voluntary contribution and its verification record. Keep any non-monetary C-Hour account distinct from costs, paid work and ecological outcomes.

The original daily reading

Preserved reading from the 14 October 2026 course, PDF page 46. The studio guide above adds practical detail; it does not replace this source.

A civic space steward attends to the living systems that support a place. Today's objective is to model one ecological relationship, make a relevant observation and compare possible interventions. The learner chooses a local system such as water, food growing, vegetation, waste, habitat or energy use.

The first brief identifies the system boundary, living participants, material flows, relevant people and the desired change. A garden may link soil, water, plants, insects, food, tools and human care. A water question may link rainfall, storage, use, drainage and ecosystem conditions. The learner gathers current sources and permissioned observations for the selected relationship.

The ecology studio adds that relationship to the community twin. A simple stock-and-flow view or scene makes dependencies visible. The learner compares two intervention options, such as changing a watering practice or repurposing a waste stream. The model records assumptions, resource demands, expected consequences and what a field observation would help resolve.

The source Big Picture Thinking calls for responsible energy, pollution recovery, potable water, ecosystem observation, food production and international collaboration. The current materials and habitat sources connect regeneration with local production and long-term settlements. Today's lesson develops one of those ideas through an actual place and measurable relationship. [S03, pp. 5-6; S12, pp. 18-20]

Technical builders connect a permitted sensor or data source where useful. Other learners inspect the system through observation, drawing and conversation with a relevant host. The group checks units, dates, calibration and the scope of the model. A source gap becomes an identified next observation rather than an invented value.

The life reflection asks how the learner participates in the system through food, water, energy, waste or care. They identify a practical change they wish to try and the resources it needs. A chosen voluntary contribution may support the community's work with its own C-hour verification. Learning, paid services and outcomes retain their distinct records.

Teaching practice introduces the relationship through an everyday encounter, such as a meal or a garden task. The newcomer follows a resource through the model and asks what changes under another condition. Evidence includes the system brief, source-linked twin layer, observation, two intervention scenarios, assumptions and next measurement. The capability developed today makes regeneration intelligible as a living network of dependencies, a foundation for resilient Earth communities and later habitat design beyond Earth.

A contributor with local experience reviews the model’s account of the system. Their knowledge may reveal a seasonal pattern, practical dependency or cultural relationship. The learner records that contribution and tests its effect on the intervention comparison.

Source: S03 Big Picture Thinking; S12 Joyful Responsible Abundance Human and SI Alignment Revised 11 October 2026.
