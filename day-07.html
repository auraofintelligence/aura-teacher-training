DAY 07 / MOVEMENT 01

Memory palaces and reusable paths

Build and teach a three-place memory route with source labels, visible ordered actions and a dependable way to return.

In this guide

PurposeWorked exampleStudio stepsTeaching and evidenceReview and accessOriginal readingNext stepPractical resources ↗

Expanded studio guide · 28 October 2026

What you will practise

Build and teach a three-place memory route with source labels, visible ordered actions and a dependable way to return.

A memory palace joins recognisable places with selected records. Remembered, recreated and imagined elements need their own labels. A reusable route adds an ordered sequence that the learner can inspect before using it. Navigation, edits and external requests have different effects, so a saved path should say what each step would actually do.

A worked example

Illustrative example: Jo imagines a workshop with a doorway for a project brief, a bench for its source photograph and a shelf for the next task. A pinned notebook would open that route. After a revision, the pin should reach the intended current record. Cards can rehearse this without implying functioning software.

Make, test and revise

  1. Select three distinct places and explain their associations. Label each remembered, recreated, imagined or blended. Attach one chosen record and its source to each place, avoiding a claim that reconstructed detail is verified memory.
  2. Write the route as ordered steps with action, target, expected result and any condition. Include opening a source and changing a reflection lens. Identify whether each step navigates, edits information or would request another service.
  3. Give the route a name and a clear starting cue, such as a doorway card or a pinned object in an available editor. Rehearse the sequence and change one step to match a revised intention.
  4. Ask a peer to follow the route with one short cue. Then revise a target record and repeat the journey. Check the version reached, the displayed sequence and the peer's ability to return independently.

Teach it to another adult

Let an adult peer explain why each stop belongs in the route and how they would return to one record. Teach a recovery cue for losing the sequence rather than taking over navigation immediately.

Keep evidence someone can inspect

  • A three-place map with memory, recreation or invention labels.
  • A named sequence distinguishing navigation, edits and proposed external actions.
  • A peer walkthrough and a repeat test after one target record changes.

Try a changed condition

Keep the underlying records but replace the imagined workshop with a familiar community venue or a spoken list. Can the learner rebuild the route while preserving its purpose and action order?

Reflect on the result

  • Which details are remembered and which were created?
  • What effect does each saved action have?
  • How does the learner recover after losing the route?

Adapt the encounter

A narrated journey or tactile sequence offers the same learning objective. Avoid motion-heavy exploration where it is unwelcome. The learner can choose an invented neutral setting instead of a personally significant place.

Take it further

Prepare a route preview that a human can inspect before replay. A builder can specify target identifiers and conditions; a creative learner can strengthen orientation through distinctive language, sound descriptions or place associations.

The original daily reading

Preserved reading from the 14 October 2026 course, PDF page 7. The studio guide above adds practical detail; it does not replace this source.

A memory palace gives selected information personally recognisable places. It may draw on a real home, a remembered landscape, an imagined library or a future workshop. Today's objective is a small route that helps the learner return to meaningful records and use them in a chosen task. The AuraOZ mind-palace proposal and the later pinned-sequence design supply the foundation. [S04, Day 4; S08, pp. 36-39, 54-59]

The learner chooses three places with distinct associations. A kitchen might hold family and food. A stage might hold music and public expression. A garden might hold care and regeneration. Each place is labelled as remembered, recreated, imagined or blended. Its story and spatial design remain linked so another person understands why it was chosen.

The studio task builds a route through those places. At each stop the learner opens a selected record, inspects its source and changes between past, present and best-self reflection. They give the route a name and save the ordered steps. A pinned object at a chosen doorway provides a way to activate the route later.

The earlier XR paper proposes capturing actions, naming and saving a sequence, restoring it, pinning a world object and replaying the interaction. Today's version makes the actions visible before a sequence changes data or requests a service. Navigation, edits and external actions have identifiable effects. The learner rehearses a favourite sequence and then modifies one step to match a changed intention.

Technical builders inspect action type, target record, time, duration, conditions and result. Creative learners shape the space's sound, colour and emotional character. Teachers examine which cues support orientation. The route is tried on a screen and in XR where available, with an equivalent voice route for learners who prefer it.

The reflection considers memory and invention together. A reconstructed room may contain details the learner remembers, details they infer and details they create for usefulness. The record retains those origins. A mind- palace encounter may open a distant memory; the learner chooses what it means and whether further exploration would be helpful.

Teaching practice asks a peer to follow the route, then explain how they would return to one item independently. The trainee offers a short cue and observes where orientation becomes difficult. Evidence includes the three- place map, its source labels, the saved action sequence and a revision prompted by the peer. This creates the course's first reusable programme: a meaningful path through information that the learner understands and is able to teach.

The saved route is tried again after one record changes. The learner checks whether the pin reaches the current version and whether its displayed steps still match the intended task. That small test connects personal continuity with dependable interaction.

Source: S04 AuraOZ Learning Progression and Thought Experiment 2017; S08 Build an Aura in Blender then in Unity XR.
