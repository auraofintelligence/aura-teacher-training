DAY 28 / MOVEMENT 03

Three observers and several meanings

Separate environment, character attention and narrator interpretation, then use a discrepancy to improve an encounter.

In this guide

PurposeWorked exampleStudio stepsTeaching and evidenceReview and accessOriginal readingNext stepPractical resources ↗

Expanded studio guide · 28 October 2026

What you will practise

Separate environment, character attention and narrator interpretation, then use a discrepancy to improve an encounter.

The three-observer arrangement in the source offers three perspectives, not three equally complete truths. The world record describes the represented environment, the character record tracks information available or noticed, and a narrator interprets visible reactions. In a simulation the author defines those boundaries; in an actual encounter the learner must not pretend to know private attention or feelings without the person’s account. Today adds that account as a separately attributed response. Keep timestamps and source roles visible. A teacher uses differences to ask a better question, rather than treating the narrator as a detector of hidden motives.

A worked example

Illustrative example: a simulated visitor pauses beside a garden map. The environment record includes a guide on a shelf behind them. Their character card says they noticed the map but not the shelf label. One narrator calls the pause confusion; another calls it interest. The fictional character’s supplied explanation is that they were checking the route’s plants. The divergent accounts create a design question without proving either narrator insightful.

Make, test and revise

  1. Create a short event in yesterday’s model. Prepare separate environment, character and narrator sheets, naming what each observer can access and which details the scenario author supplied.
  2. Run the event and record observations before sharing the character explanation. Mark statements such as 'looked towards the map' separately from interpretations such as 'seemed uncertain'.
  3. Compare the records and choose one useful discrepancy. Revise a cue or add an optional explanation, then repeat the encounter with its changed condition clearly recorded.
  4. Ask a second narrator to describe the same event. Retain both readings and write one neutral question that would let the person explain what the pause meant to them.

Teach it to another adult

Present the streams without announcing a preferred interpretation. Ask the peer what each observer could know and which question would reduce uncertainty without steering the answer.

Keep evidence someone can inspect

  • Three attributed observer streams with their access boundaries stated.
  • The character’s separately labelled account and a comparison of divergences.
  • One revised encounter and a neutral clarification question.

Try a changed condition

Use the method on a fictional collaborative design meeting. Have a peer distinguish a missed instruction, a practical obstacle and an unverified emotional interpretation before proposing support.

Reflect on the result

  • Which statement describes an event and which supplies its meaning?
  • What does the character’s account change about the narrator’s conclusion?
  • Could the proposed clarification invite a different answer from the one expected?

Adapt the encounter

Use written transcripts, audio description or simple sequence cards. Learners may analyse a fictional scene rather than disclose or re-enact a personal misunderstanding.

Take it further

Compare two interface revisions against the same starting scene. Record whether task completion and the character’s own experience point towards the same improvement.

The original daily reading

Preserved reading from the 14 October 2026 course, PDF page 28. The studio guide above adds practical detail; it does not replace this source.

The life-simulation source proposes three observers running alongside the main process: the whole environment, the portion noticed by the character and a narrator observing the character's reactions. Today's objective is to keep those accounts separately attributed, compare their meanings and use the differences to improve an encounter. [S10, pp. 28-32]

The learner uses yesterday's scene. A visitor enters a community hub looking for a resource. The environment model knows the layout, signs, equipment and rules. The character attends to some cues and misses others. The narrator describes the interaction, including pauses and actions. The learner then adds the character's own explanation of what they meant and experienced.

The studio task records these streams during a short simulation. The class identifies where the accounts agree and where they diverge. A sign exists in the world model but falls outside the character's attention. A pause appears uncertain to the narrator while the character describes enjoying the view. The system keeps each account linked to its source and time.

Technical builders implement separate observer records and a comparison view. Creative learners write alternative narrator interpretations. Teachers examine whether an observed difficulty arose from the interface, prior knowledge, access conditions or the explanation supplied. Civic learners identify how an outside interpretation might affect a decision about someone else's needs.

The scene is revised using one informative difference. A cue becomes more visible, an explanation becomes clearer or a resource receives another access route. The learner repeats the encounter and compares the observations. The revised design receives evidence from both the task result and the person's account.

The life reflection connects this work with a remembered misunderstanding. The learner considers what they knew, what another person observed and what each inferred. They describe a question that might have improved the encounter. That question becomes a reusable dialogue cue in the Aura.

Teaching practice presents the three accounts to a peer without immediately explaining the intended interpretation. The peer identifies what each observer is able to know and asks the character a clarifying question. The trainee checks whether the exercise encourages curiosity and source awareness. Evidence includes the observer streams, comparison, chosen revision, repeated encounter and teaching feedback. This is a key preparation for socialising Auras: another mind's visible actions, available information and internal meaning meet through dialogue and remain open to revision.

The group repeats the exercise with a different narrator. Comparing their interpretations reveals which details belong to the observed event and which depend on viewpoint. The learner then writes a question that would help clarify the character’s own meaning.

Source: S10 MoE Extended.
