DAY 03 / MOVEMENT 01

Past present and best selves

Represent past, present and several possible best selves with their circumstances, resources and distinct meanings intact.

In this guide

PurposeWorked exampleStudio stepsTeaching and evidenceReview and accessOriginal readingNext stepPractical resources ↗

Expanded studio guide · 28 October 2026

What you will practise

Represent past, present and several possible best selves with their circumstances, resources and distinct meanings intact.

A remembered action, today's interpretation and a preferred future response answer different questions. Keeping them linked without merging them lets a person change their understanding while preserving context. A best self is conditional: the support available in a quiet rehearsal differs from that available during a busy public event. Several preferred responses can therefore coexist.

A worked example

Illustrative example: Alex recalls declining an invitation to perform music. Today Alex interprets the choice as needing preparation. A preferred future response might be a short performance with a trusted accompanist, while another best-self scenario involves listening and supporting someone else. Neither imagined version changes the remembered account into evidence that a performance occurred.

Make, test and revise

  1. Choose an everyday event or a fictional scenario with manageable emotional weight. Make separate records for remembered action, current interpretation and preferred response. Label each perspective and its time clearly.
  2. Describe the surroundings, expectations, inner wording, available resources and perceived options for each account. Add a second best-self condition that changes one resource, such as preparation time or support from another person.
  3. Ask a peer or available model to summarise the relationships. Mark any confusion between desire and action, or past circumstances and present understanding. Save the original summary beside the person's correction.
  4. Choose a small outside action or a rehearsal related to the preferred response. Record the intention before trying it, then describe what actually happened and which condition mattered. A planned rehearsal remains a plan.

Teach it to another adult

Invite an adult peer to explain why two preferred responses could both fit the same person. Reflect their interpretation back without deciding which account is the person's true identity.

Keep evidence someone can inspect

  • Three linked perspectives with explicit time and context labels.
  • A summary correction separating intention, action and interpretation.
  • A small action or rehearsal record showing its conditions and next question.

Try a changed condition

Remove a resource from one best-self scenario. Ask the learner to revise the preferred response without rewriting the past or treating the changed plan as a personal failure.

Reflect on the result

  • Where did present knowledge enter the remembered story?
  • Which resource changed the available response?
  • What would help distinguish two interpretations?

Adapt the encounter

Offer third-person fiction, drawing or a brief spoken account. The learner can choose an ordinary event and decline deeper exploration. The exercise is reflection and teaching practice, not a demand for trauma disclosure.

Take it further

For a builder route, design separate perspective and event identifiers. For a creative route, present the same scene from two time perspectives while preserving the distinction between memory and imagined possibility.

The original daily reading

Preserved reading from the 14 October 2026 course, PDF page 3. The studio guide above adds practical detail; it does not replace this source.

Self-reflection gains depth when the same category is examined from several positions. The learner works with past self, present self and best self or best selves under defined conditions. A best self in a familiar home, an uncertain workplace, a creative performance or a crisis may need different resources and qualities. These perspectives help the Aura represent a person whose preferences develop through experience.

Today's chosen category might be friendship. The learner recalls a past friendship, describes what they value in friendship now and imagines how they would like to show up in several circumstances. They examine the exterior environment, cultural and legal expectations, internal monologue, known resources and perceived resourcefulness around each story. The comparison shows how context influences action.

The studio task creates three linked accounts of a personally significant event. The first describes what the learner remembers doing. The second describes how they interpret it now. The third explores a preferred response under specified conditions. A fourth record may hold an alternative best self, such as a courageous public response and a considerate private response. Each retains its own wording, time perspective and assumptions.

A model-assisted dialogue offers questions and summaries. The learner checks whether the model has confused intention with action, desire with resources or present interpretation with past experience. Corrections become examples for later personal-model work. Technical learners examine how the prompt and selected records changed the response. Philosophical learners examine the assumptions carried by the questions.

The outside-life practice is a small action related to the chosen best self. Someone reflecting on friendship might arrange a conversation. Someone exploring creative confidence might share a short draft with a selected person. The learner records the intended action, what happened and what they learned about the circumstances. The next reflection then has lived material to work with.

Teachers practise allowing more than one coherent account. A learner may recognise an earlier choice as protective, wish for a different outcome and still value the person they were. The teacher returns the relationships among those ideas rather than forcing a single verdict. Their questions invite detail about resources, consequences and desired next steps.

Evidence for today includes the linked selves, the model's original summary, the learner's correction and one outside action or planned rehearsal. A peer asks whether the records make the person's context understandable. This exercise begins the course's alignment theme: examining how choices meet values under actual conditions. It also prepares later simulations, where a character's situation and the observer's knowledge will differ. [S04, abstract and Day 1; S12, pp. 4, 10-11]
